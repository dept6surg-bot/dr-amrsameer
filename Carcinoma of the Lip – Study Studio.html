Carcinoma of the lip

Anatomy, pathology, staging and treatment as taught in your lecture, with infographics, flashcards, a quick true/false quiz and a 15-question MCQ exam. For study, not patient care.

1. Where it happens

Lower lip
93%
Upper lip
5%
Angle of mouth
2%

Lower lip: at the junction of the middle and outer thirds. The upper lip is the common site for a syphilitic chancre. Lower lip is mostly squamous cell carcinoma; upper lip is where basal cell carcinoma is commoner.

3. Pathology and spread

Naked-eye types

Exophytic: malignant nodule (may ulcerate); malignant ulcer (commonest).

Endophytic: diffuse infiltrating "woody lip"; indurated malignant fissure.

Microscopy

SCC (cell nests, cell pearls): lower lip.

BCC: upper lip.

Rare: melanoma, sarcoma, salivary gland tumours, myoblastoma, pyogenic granuloma, keratoacanthoma, granulomatous cheilitis.

Spread

Direct: within the lip; late to cheek, gum, mandible.

Lymphatic: slow, late, rare (about 10%).

Blood: extremely late and rare.

5. Anatomy at a glance

Sensory

Upper lip: infraorbital (V2)

Commissure: buccal (V3)

Lower lip: mental (V3)

Motor (facial nerve)

Upper lip: buccal branch

Lower lip: marginal mandibular branch

Blood supply

Superior and inferior labial arteries from the facial artery form a circumoral arcade at the vermilion border.

Veins drain to the anterior facial vein.

6. Clinical picture

Patient

Over 50 years, male more than female.

Rapidly growing, single ulcer: raised, everted edge; necrotic floor; indurated base extending beyond the ulcer, may be fixed.

Nodes if enlarged

Infective: firm, painful.

Metastatic: hard, painless; mobile early, fixed late.

Numb lower lip along the mental nerve means growth up the nerve into the mandible.

Complications

  • Fungation and ulceration
  • Haemorrhage and infection
  • Dysarthria and dysphagia
  • Distant metastases
Biopsy is mandatory: hyperkeratosis, cheilitis and keratoacanthoma can mimic carcinoma. Punch, incision or excision biopsy of the lesion; FNAC for suspicious nodes; X-ray or CT of neck and mandible to judge resectability.

8. Treatment of the primary

Stage I–II

Surgery alone, or radiotherapy alone, or surgery then radiotherapy.

Stage III–IV

Surgery then radiotherapy, or palliative radiotherapy, or palliative chemotherapy (mitomycin C).

Margins (full thickness, frozen section)

Stage I–II: 5–8 mm

Stage III–IV: 1–2 cm

Vermilionectomy (lip shave)

Superficial or multicentric mucosal carcinoma, and premalignant lesions.

Mohs surgery needs ALL of

Stage I–II, well differentiated, thickness 2.5 mm or less, no muscle involvement.

Surgery preferred when

Under 50% of lip; bone infiltration; radio-recurrent or radioresistant; cancer on syphilitic scar; young patient.

10. Radiotherapy and neck nodes

Radiotherapy

Instead of surgery: early lesion under 50% of lip, early commissure lesion (I–II), inoperable or unfit.

After surgery: stage III–IV, recurrence, extracapsular spread, multilevel nodes, more than two positive nodes, perineural invasion, undissected N0 contralateral neck.

Lecture doses: interstitial radium needles 7000 rad over 7 days; external beam 5000 rad over 15 days.

No palpable nodes

Monthly follow-up. Prophylactic supraomohyoid dissection if: tumour over 3 cm (especially commissure), local recurrence, thickness over 5 mm, undifferentiated, perineural invasion.

Palpable nodes

Supraomohyoid dissection is enough, unless upper deep cervical nodes are involved or the tumour is highly malignant: then modified radical or radical neck dissection.

Prognosis is good except with nodal involvement and with carcinoma at the angle of the mouth (double lymphatic drainage, up and down). Inoperable: unfit, distant metastases, or unresectable infiltration; give palliative radiotherapy or chemotherapy.
